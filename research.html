Research

My research program centers on one question: how can AI tell whether information is still appropriate to use now? My dissertation develops a framework for characterizing temporal change in knowledge-grounded AI, supported by work in explainable AI, medical vision-language systems, and video understanding.

Dissertation Research

Characterizing Temporal Change for Reliable Knowledge-Grounded AI. AI systems such as large language models increasingly rely on external information to answer questions. A piece of information can still be related to a question yet no longer be appropriate for the current situation. This research treats timeliness as distinct from recency: older information may remain useful — or become useful again when conditions recur — and the newest information is not always the most appropriate.

The dissertation hypothesizes that temporal change is not a single phenomenon but may comprise several partly independent kinds, and tests which detector best captures each, rather than assuming any single signal is sufficient:

  • Structural change — detected with topological data analysis: change in how information is organized relative to the environment.
  • Semantic change — detected with embedding drift: drift in the meaning and representation of information over time.
  • Distributional change — detected with Wasserstein/MMD shift metrics: statistical distribution shift away from the present environment.
  • Validity / volatility — detected with validity horizons: whether information is still valid at query time, and how fast it changes.

Recency (age alone) is used only as a comparison baseline — newer is not assumed better. Each detector is validated independently before any combination, and signals are combined adaptively only where the evidence shows it helps.

Core Research Questions

  1. Does temporal appropriateness measurably affect LLM response reliability? Establishing that current, outdated, mixed, and conflicting information change model outputs.
  2. Are the kinds of temporal change genuinely distinct? Testing whether structural, semantic, distributional, and validity/volatility change overlap or capture different phenomena.
  3. Which detector best captures which kind of change? Matching TDA, embedding drift, distribution-shift metrics, and validity modeling to change types across domains.
  4. Does adaptive, matched detection outperform single signals and recency? Comparing adaptive combination against any single detector, blind combination, and recency baselines.
  5. Does timeliness-aware retrieval improve accuracy and reliability? Testing appropriateness-aware selection against semantic retrieval, with explainability for why it helps.
  6. How does timeliness sensitivity differ across architectures? Comparing neural networks, transformers, and LLMs under the same temporal-change conditions.
