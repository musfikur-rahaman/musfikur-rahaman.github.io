Research

My research program centers on one question: how can AI tell whether information is still appropriate to use now? The dissertation develops a framework for characterizing temporal change in knowledge-grounded AI, supported by work in explainable AI, medical vision-language systems, and video understanding.

Dissertation Research

Characterizing Temporal Change for Reliable Knowledge-Grounded AI

AI systems such as large language models increasingly rely on external information to answer questions. A piece of information can still be related to a question yet no longer be appropriate for the current situation. This research treats timeliness as distinct from recency: older information may remain useful — or become useful again when conditions recur — and the newest information is not always the most appropriate.

The dissertation hypothesizes that temporal change is not a single phenomenon but may comprise several partly independent kinds — structural, semantic, distributional, and validity/volatility — and tests which detector best captures each, rather than assuming any single signal is sufficient:

Structural

Topological Data Analysis

Change in how information is organized relative to the environment.

Semantic

Embedding Drift

Drift in the meaning and representation of information over time.

Distributional

Wasserstein / MMD

Statistical distribution shift away from the present environment.

Validity / Volatility

Validity Horizons

Whether information is still valid at query time, and how fast it changes.

Recency (age alone) is used only as a comparison baseline — newer is not assumed better. Each detector is validated independently before any combination, and signals are combined adaptively only where the evidence shows it helps.

Core Research Questions

  • Does temporal appropriateness measurably affect LLM response reliability?

    Establishing that current, outdated, mixed, and conflicting information change model outputs.

  • Are the four kinds of temporal change genuinely distinct?

    Testing whether structural, semantic, distributional, and validity/volatility change overlap or capture different phenomena.

  • Which detector best captures which kind of change?

    Matching TDA, embedding drift, distribution-shift metrics, and validity modeling to change types across domains.

  • Does adaptive, matched detection outperform single signals and recency?

    Comparing adaptive combination against any single detector, blind combination, and recency baselines.

  • Does timeliness-aware retrieval improve accuracy and reliability?

    Testing appropriateness-aware selection against semantic retrieval, with explainability for why it helps.

  • How does timeliness sensitivity differ across architectures?

    Comparing neural networks, transformers, and LLMs under the same temporal-change conditions.
